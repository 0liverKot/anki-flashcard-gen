Topic 5: Waves and Particle Nature of Light



# 5.59 Definitions

# 5.59 Definitions

Amplitude: A wave’s maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.

Frequency, f: The number of complete oscillations passing through a point per second.

Period, T: The time taken for one complete oscillation.

Speed, v: The distance travelled by the wave per unit time.

Wavelength, λ: The length of one complete oscillation, such as the distance between successive peaks or troughs.

# 5.60 Wave Equation

Wave speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength.

v = fλ

# 5.61 Longitudinal Waves

In a longitudinal wave, particles oscillate parallel to the direction of energy transfer.

- The wave consists of compressions and rarefactions.
- Mechanical longitudinal waves cannot travel through a vacuum.
- Sound is a longitudinal wave.
- A longitudinal wave can be demonstrated by pushing and pulling a slinky horizontally.

## Compressions and rarefactions

Rarefaction: A region of decreased pressure where neighbouring particles move farther apart.

Compression: A region of increased pressure where neighbouring particles move closer together.

# 5.62 Transverse Waves

In a transverse wave, particles or fields oscillate at right angles to the direction of energy transfer.

- All electromagnetic waves are transverse.
- Electromagnetic waves travel at 3.0 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum.
- Transverse waves can be demonstrated using vertical oscillations of a slinky or waves on a string.

# 5.63 Graphs of Waves

## Displacement-distance graphs

These show how particle displacement varies with distance along a wave. They can be used to measure wavelength.

- For a transverse wave, the graph resembles the shape of the wave.
- For a longitudinal wave, the graph represents displacement rather than the physical arrangement of the medium.

## Displacement-time graphs

These show how the displacement of one particle varies with time. They can be used to measure the period.

A stationary wave can also be represented on a displacement-distance graph.

# 5.65 Further Definitions

Phase: The position of a point within a wave cycle. It may be measured in radians, degrees or fractions of a cycle.

Phase difference: How far one particle or wave lags behind another, measured in radians, degrees or fractions of a cycle.

Path difference: The difference between the distances travelled by two waves.

Superposition: When waves overlap, the resultant displacement is the vector sum of their individual displacements.

Coherence: Coherent waves have the same frequency and wavelength and maintain a fixed phase difference.

Wavefront: A surface joining points that are at the same phase.

## Interference

Constructive interference: Waves meet in phase, so their displacements add.

Destructive interference: Waves meet completely out of phase, so their displacements subtract.

# 5.66 Phase Difference and Path Difference

Two coherent waves are in phase when their phase difference is an integer multiple of 360° or 2π radians. They need not have the same amplitude.

Two coherent waves are completely out of phase when their phase difference is an odd integer multiple of 180° or π radians.

Δx = (λ / 2π)Δφ

Here, Δx is path difference, λ is wavelength and Δφ is phase difference in radians.

## Example

For a path difference of 6 m and wavelength of 2 m:

Δφ = 2π(Δx / λ) = 2π(6 / 2) = 6π

Because 6π is a multiple of 2π, the waves are in phase.

# 5.67 Stationary Waves

A stationary or standing wave forms through the superposition of two progressive waves travelling in opposite directions in the same plane, with the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude.

A stationary wave transfers no net energy.

Antinode: A region of maximum displacement formed where the waves interfere constructively.

Node: A region of zero displacement formed where the waves interfere destructively.

## Formation on a string

- A wave travels from an oscillator along the string.
- The wave reflects at the fixed end and travels back along the string.
- The incident and reflected waves superpose.
- When their frequency, wavelength and amplitude match, a stationary wave forms.

# 5.68 Speed of a Transverse Wave on a String

v = √(T / μ)

Here, v is wave speed, T is tension and μ is mass per unit length.

# 5.70 Intensity of Radiation

Intensity is power, or energy transferred per unit time, per unit area.

I = P / A

Here, P is power and A is area.

# 5.71 Refractive Index and Snell’s Law

Refractive index measures how much a material slows light relative to its speed in a vacuum.

n = c / v

Here, c is the speed of light in a vacuum and v is the speed of light in the material. A higher refractive index indicates a more optically dense material.

Refraction occurs when a wave enters another medium and changes speed, usually changing direction.

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

- n₁ and n₂ are the refractive indices of the two materials.
- θ₁ is the angle of incidence, measured from the normal.
- θ₂ is the angle of refraction, measured from the normal.
- Light entering a more optically dense material slows and bends towards the normal.
- Light entering a less optically dense material speeds up and bends away from the normal.

# 5.72 Critical Angle

As the angle of incidence increases, the angle of refraction increases. The critical angle, C, is the angle of incidence that produces an angle of refraction of exactly 90°, so the refracted ray travels along the boundary.

For a material of refractive index n next to air:

sin C = 1 / n where n > 1

# 5.73 Total Internal Reflection

Total internal reflection occurs when both conditions are met:

- The wave travels from a material of higher refractive index to one of lower refractive index.
- The angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

# 5.74 Measuring the Refractive Index of a Solid

- Place the material block in the centre of a sheet of paper and draw around it.
- Remove the block. Mark a point on its outline and draw a normal at exactly 90° to the boundary.
- Use a protractor to draw incident-ray lines at 10° intervals from 10° to 70°, measured from the normal.
- Replace the block carefully within its outline.
- Direct a ray-box beam along the 10° incident line and mark where it leaves the block.
- Join the exit point to the entry point and measure the angle between this line and the normal. This is the angle of refraction.
- Repeat for all incident angles.
- Repeat the experiment twice more and calculate the mean refracted angle for each incident angle.
- Plot sin i on the vertical axis against sin r on the horizontal axis. The gradient of the best-fit line is the refractive index.

## Derivation

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

For light travelling from air, n₁ ≈ 1. Replacing θ₁ with i, θ₂ with r and n₂ with n gives:

sin i = n sin r

y = mx

Therefore, for a graph of sin i against sin r, the gradient equals n.

# 5.75 Focal Length of Lenses

Converging lens: Curves outwards and causes parallel rays to converge at a point.

Diverging lens: Curves inwards and causes parallel rays to diverge.

Principal focus, F: For a converging lens, the point where rays parallel to the principal axis meet. For a diverging lens, the point from which the rays appear to originate.

Focal length, f: The distance from the optical centre of the lens to the principal focus.

Power: A measure of the lens’s ability to bend light. It is positive for converging lenses and negative for diverging lenses.

# 5.76 Ray Diagrams

- From one point on the object, draw a ray through the optical centre of the lens. It continues undeviated.
- From the same point, draw a ray parallel to the principal axis. After the lens, it passes through the principal focus for a converging lens.
- A real image forms where the rays meet.
- For a virtual image, extend the refracted rays backwards using construction lines. The image appears where these extensions meet.

# 5.77 Power of a Lens

P = 1 / f

Focal length f is measured in metres and power P is measured in dioptres, D. Power is positive for a converging lens and negative for a diverging lens.

# 5.78 Thin Lenses in Combination

Thin lenses used together act as one lens whose power is the sum of the individual powers.

P = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …

# 5.79 Real and Virtual Images

Real image: An image that can be projected onto a screen because light rays reach the image position.

Virtual image: An image that cannot be projected onto a screen because light rays do not pass through the apparent image position.

# 5.80 Measuring the Power of a Lens

For a thin converging or diverging lens:

1/u + 1/v = 1/f = P

- u is the distance between the object and the lens.
- v is the distance between the lens and the image; v is positive for a real image and negative for a virtual image.
- f is the focal length.

# 5.81 Magnification

Magnification is the ratio of image size to object size.

m = image height / object height = v / u

# 5.82 Plane Polarisation

A polarised wave oscillates in one plane only. Only transverse waves can be polarised.

- A wave aligned with a polarising filter’s transmission axis passes through.
- A wave perpendicular to the transmission axis is blocked.
- Polarised sunglasses reduce glare by blocking components of partially polarised light reflected from surfaces such as water and tarmac.

# 5.83 Diffraction and Huygens’ Construction

Diffraction: The spreading of waves as they pass through a gap or around an obstacle.

Huygens’ construction states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets. These wavelets spread out and form the next wavefront.

- Sound diffracts strongly through a doorway because its wavelength is comparable with the doorway width.
- Visible light diffracts much less through a doorway because its wavelength is far smaller than the gap.
- Diffraction is greatest when the gap size is similar to the wavelength.

# 5.84 Diffraction Grating Equation

A diffraction grating contains many equally spaced, closely separated slits. Light passing through produces bright and dark interference fringes.

- The central maximum is the zero-order line.
- The next maxima are first order, then second order, and so on.

d sin θ = nλ

- d is the slit spacing.
- θ is the angle from the normal to the maximum.
- n is the order number.
- λ is the wavelength.

# 5.86 Electron Diffraction

In an electron-diffraction experiment, an electron gun accelerates electrons through a vacuum towards a crystal lattice. The electrons interact with the small gaps between atoms and produce a concentric-ring interference pattern on a fluorescent screen.

If electrons behaved only as particles, a single spot would be expected. Diffraction is a wave behaviour, so the observed pattern is evidence for the wave nature of electrons.

# 5.87 de Broglie Relation

The de Broglie hypothesis states that all particles have both wave and particle properties.

λ = h / p

Here, λ is de Broglie wavelength, h is the Planck constant and p is momentum.

# 5.88 Wave Behaviour at an Interface

Interface: A boundary between two materials.

Transmission: The wave passes into the next material and may refract if its speed changes.

Reflection: The wave returns from the boundary without passing into the next material.

# 5.89 Pulse-Echo Technique

Pulse-echo imaging uses ultrasound, with frequency greater than 20 kHz, and reflections from boundaries between materials.

- Transmit a short ultrasound pulse into the target.
- The pulse travels until it reaches a boundary, where part of it is reflected. A larger density difference produces a stronger reflection.
- Detect the returning reflected waves.
- Use reflected intensity to infer structure and return time to determine position.

s = vt

Long pulses may overlap and reduce resolution. Increasing wavelength also reduces the ability to resolve fine detail.

# 5.90 Wave and Photon Models of Electromagnetic Radiation

The wave model describes electromagnetic radiation as transverse waves. The photon model describes it as discrete packets called photons. Light displays both wave and particle behaviour, which is called wave-particle duality.

E = hf

# 5.91 Photon Energy

Photon energy is directly proportional to frequency.

E = hf

Here, E is photon energy, h is the Planck constant and f is frequency.

# 5.92 Photoelectricity

The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons from a metal surface when electromagnetic radiation above a threshold frequency is incident on it.

A surface electron absorbs one photon. If the gained energy is sufficient, the electron leaves the metal as a photoelectron.

# 5.93 Threshold Frequency, Work Function and Photoelectric Equation

Threshold frequency: The minimum frequency of incident radiation that can cause photoelectron emission from a particular metal.

Work function, Φ: The minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface of a metal.

E = hf = Φ + Eₖ(max)

The photon energy is used to overcome the work function; any remaining energy becomes the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectron.

# 5.94 Electronvolt

The electronvolt is a unit of energy suitable for small energies. One electronvolt is the kinetic energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V.

1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

- To convert joules to electronvolts, divide by 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹.
- To convert electronvolts to joules, multiply by 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹.

# 5.95 Photoelectric Effect as Evidence for the Photon Model

## Observations not explained by a simple wave model

- A threshold frequency exists. Radiation below it produces no emission, regardless of exposure time.
- Emission occurs immediately when suitable radiation reaches the metal.
- Increasing intensity increases the number of photoelectrons emitted per second, not their maximum kinetic energy.
- Photoelectrons leave with a range of kinetic energies.

## Photon-model explanation

- A photon transfers all its energy to one electron. If hf ≥ Φ, the electron may escape.
- Energy transfer occurs in a single interaction, explaining immediate emission.
- Greater intensity means more photons arrive each second, so more electrons can be emitted each second.
- Electrons deeper in the metal lose different amounts of energy through collisions before escaping, producing a range of kinetic energies.

At threshold: hf₀ = Φ

# 5.96 Atomic Line Spectra

Electrons in atoms occupy discrete energy levels. An electron can absorb energy and move to a higher level. When it returns to a lower level, it emits a photon.

Passing light from excited gas atoms through a diffraction grating or prism produces a line spectrum. Each line corresponds to a particular wavelength and photon energy, providing evidence for discrete atomic energy levels.

The energy of an emitted or absorbed photon equals the difference between two energy levels.

ΔE = E₁ - E₂

ΔE = hf = E₁ - E₂

f = (E₁ - E₂) / h
